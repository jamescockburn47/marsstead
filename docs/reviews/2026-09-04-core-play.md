# Marsstead core play review — 4 September 2026

Read-only code and design review by an independent reviewer. No production changes. Findings distinguish executable behaviour from intended design; this report does not claim observed child playtesting. Source paths and line numbers refer to the reviewed working tree.

## Overall judgment

The interesting foundation is already here: a buggy with genuine handling, digging that produces construction resources, a spatial home-design puzzle, solar/storage planning, persistent marks on Mars, salvage at historical missions, and a spectacular flight-ready ship available from the start. However, the player currently encounters an obstructed manufacturing chain, manual inventory friction, counters with weak consequences, and relatively few reasons to express themselves. Fixing connectivity and making every short expedition change the home will buy more fun than adding another simulation layer.

The design has evolved. `docs/DESIGN.md` describes panel-built surface homes; `docs/STRUCTURE.md` explicitly replaces the primary home with an underground schematic and says players never walk the hab. A walkable interior is consequently a proposed doctrine change, not a missing implementation requirement. The existing console home can become much more tangible without reversing that decision.

## Verified blockers and misleading rules

### 1. The main manufacturing progression cannot be built through ordinary inventory

`src/main.js:1542-1550` determines machine affordability from the suit alone; `src/main.js:1569-1575` removes costs from that same suit. Suit capacity is 35 kg (`src/inventory.js:39`). Standard recipe masses, computed directly from the exported catalogues:

| Machine | Required carried mass | Can fit in suit? |
|---|---:|---|
| Smelter | 52 kg | No |
| Electrolyser | 38 kg | No |
| Solar array | 52 kg | No; salvaged 18 kg solar-wing alternative fits |
| Battery | 32 kg | Yes |
| Mill | 58 kg | No |
| Assembler | 56 kg | No |

Recipes are in `src/machines.js:19-79`; steel panels weigh 26 kg (`src/inventory.js:26`). Thus even a player with all the ingredients on the adjacent rover cannot build the mill/assembler needed for renewable fuel production. The merged lander/hopper is already flight-ready with three tanks at landfall (`src/main.js:369-377`): this blocks the industry that sustains later flights, not initial flight access. This is a progression defect, not difficulty. The rover is explicitly supposed to carry a build's worth (`src/inventory.js:37-40`) and already supplies other transactions such as ring installation (`src/main.js:302-323`).

Recommendation: one consistent nearby-worksite material pool for preview, affordability, debit and refunds. Preserve mass limits for expeditions while allowing a parked rover/stockpile to supply construction. Show the eligible source and precise shortage in the placement preview. Test a fresh-save route through array, smelter, mill, assembler and production/loading of replacement ship fuel using actual capacity constraints.

### 2. The promised unattended refining chain stops at ore

VESPER's mechanics corpus explicitly promises that spoil becomes steel unattended (`src/gamefacts.js:44`). The landfall orders describe the spoil→steel→array loop but do not use that word (`src/orders.js:68-75`). In the implementation, `src/refine.js:34-43` drops output into the tray and never refeeds it. `src/main.js:1333-1362` only takes output and feeds the next recipe on another T interaction. The frame tick at `src/main.js:2920-2925` does no pipeline transfer.

Reproduction using pure exported functions: feed one regolith, tick the fabricator for 120 seconds in 0.05-second steps. Result: `queue: []`, `out: { 'iron-ore': 1 }`; no steel. `scripts/verify-refine.mjs:33-45` performs an extra automatic take/refeed inside the test and thereby supplies behaviour the unattended product does not have.

Recommendation: a selectable desired output, e.g. “Make 2 steel”, which queues and executes the permitted regolith→ore→steel chain explicitly. Stop there, preserving intermediate goods where desired. A single “collect all to nearby storage” action should move finished output. The read-only Works console (`src/worksconsole.js:1-7`) can remain a planning instrument, but the actual bench interaction should not require repeated unexplained T presses.

### 3. Survival promises are not implemented as stated

`src/gamefacts.js:50-57` explicitly tells players that exhaustion causes a blackout, dropped cargo and awakening at shelter, and even recommends letting the suit run out if stranded. The reviewed `src/` contains no blackout/rescue implementation. The suit tick (`src/main.js:3071-3085`) clamps air at zero and warns once; it does not rescue or change movement.

Warmth is even less consequential: `surfaceTempC()` is bounded at -84 °C (`src/marslight.js:136-139`), while the worst-case warmth update is `0.05 - 0.9*0.02 = +0.032` per second (`src/main.js:3080-3082`). The suit therefore warms even on the coldest modeled night. This is an exact code consequence, not a judgment about how severe cold ought to be.

`everPressurised` also only changes in the legacy surface-building flood-fill (`src/main.js:1663-1672`), whereas the intended burrow-home transition (`src/main.js:2977-2990`) does not set it. The default intended route consequently retains the initial eight-hour bottle rather than the described five-hour post-home bottle. At TIME_SCALE 40 (`src/main.js:153`), those are 12 and 7.5 real minutes respectively before rested bonuses.

Recommendation: choose and implement an honest, forgiving survival contract. Clearly signposted low-air return guidance, an always-available call-home/rescue, short recovery and recoverable cargo are sufficient. Do not fix this by merely adding harsher drains. Restore trust first; then tune independent assistance settings. In a relaxed setting, maintain the excitement through weather and expedition choices while suppressing loss.

### 4. Some apparent production choices are currently dead ends

Ice becomes water (`src/refine.js:17`, `src/machines.js:31-36`), but no water-consuming gameplay operation exists in the reviewed `src/`. The assembler makes a winch rig (`src/machines.js:77`), but it has no consuming action yet. Methane fuel instead comes straight from machine parts (`src/machines.js:76`). An eight-year-old should not be expected to distinguish implemented utility from future placeholders by experiment.

Recommendation: keep unimplemented products out of the actionable progression palette or clearly mark them as forthcoming. When adding utility, connect ice/water to one satisfying visible result (first crop, coolant/fuel supply or an expedition provision), rather than introducing several new meters at once.

### 5. Deconstructing a working machine discards its contents

`src/main.js:1582-1598` refunds construction materials then removes the machine without accounting for its queue or output tray. This can delete stored player work. It also refunds the standard recipe even when a solar wing paid the alternate cost, so salvage can change into steel through deconstruction. Those are additional reasons to centralise construction transactions. Empty/discharge contents safely before removal, or reject removal with an explicit recoverable explanation; preserve original paid cost when a refund is intended.

## Why the working systems can still feel boring

1. **Early progress is mainly waiting and translation.** Initial charge is 4 kWh; a shaft costs 4, a bench 6, and the initial bank holds 6 (`src/power.js:37-42,107-115`). With an idle 1 kW RTG and TIME_SCALE 40, a depleted bank takes nine real minutes to reach a bench's six kWh. Cooking leaves only 0.2 kW of baseline surplus before other loads. Solar is the escape from this wait, but its normal recipe is presently inaccessible. Salvage is additionally locked until sleeping through a night (`src/main.js:1365-1369,2352-2363`). The opening needs a clearly purposeful activity during that period, not just an attractive sunset and instructions.
2. **Handling material is not yet an interesting logistics choice.** A suit carries one steel panel; G takes the heaviest item that fits, not an item the player chooses (`src/main.js:1477-1485`). Workbench output goes to suit only, although input may come from the rover (`src/main.js:1333-1362`). This produces repeated unloading and surprising item selection. The interesting question is “what shall I bring on this trip?”; the repeated chore is “how do I get the required item out of my own nearby bag?”
3. **The home has several useful numbers but little expressive range.** The burrow catalogue contains shaft, corridor, bunk, store, bay and garden; depth/adjacency give shelter, air and haul scores (`src/burrow.js:213-239`). That is a real puzzle worth retaining. But shelter uses the best bunk, haul caps after two qualifying stores, and two lit gardens already cap air. Beyond those optima, extra rooms mostly consume power and produce more spoil. The Works Bay has a bunk-noise penalty but no compensating production benefit in this core. Long-term building needs visible personal or functional purposes.
4. **Resource expeditions offer too few interactions per trip.** Deposits are deterministic on a 260 m candidate grid, 22% chance, absent within 240 m of home; rig deployment is within 14 m and maximum slope 0.18 (`src/mine.js:10-17,73-76`). Drilling then yields one unit each 18 seconds and stops at eight. The hauling decision is sound. The dig itself is a timer. Complement it with optional observation, routing and a nearby discovery so the player is doing something meaningful while the drill works.
5. **The strongest toy lacks a play structure around it.** The buggy has handbrake drift, air pitch/roll, flip and clean-landing flags, self-righting and recall (`src/buggy.js:28-29,68-79,559-599`). This is not merely transportation. Routes, delivery challenges and photo-worthy jumps can make the world fun without combat, another resource type or fictional wildlife.
6. **Flight gives a spectacle but little activity during the trip.** The ship is available from landfall; range responds to payload, and flight lasts roughly 39–65 seconds depending on distance and is deliberately cinematic (`src/main.js:369-377`; `src/hopper.js:99-121`). Preserve the spectacle. Give the planning a concrete purpose and let repeated flights offer a fast travel/shortened sequence option after the first successful route, unless maintaining its full duration is central to a chosen mode. Do not convert it to free flight merely to add inputs; the current architectural doctrine explicitly avoids that.

An inexpensive deterministic pacing probe used the real power/burrow functions, the same tick order as main, 0.05-second steps, three drones, initial four kWh, no arrays or cooking, and immediate planning of shaft→corridor→bunk as each predecessor finished. The shaft finished at 29.8 real seconds; corridor at 323.8 seconds; bunk at 908.8 seconds (15 min 8.8 sec). This is an idealised baseline, not a human playthrough: ring hauling/installation, UI time and competing production were excluded, while ring installation was deferred to avoid comfort load. It demonstrates that the first-home path is dominated by charge accrual even when the player makes every planning action instantly. Parallel exploration can turn that time into play, but the current game must actually teach and reward those excursions.

## A first playable slice worth building

Target one excellent 25–35-minute landing-to-home-to-expedition session before filling a nominal twenty-hour campaign. Timing targets below are design hypotheses for testing, not claims about children's attention spans.

| Time | Player action | Immediate payoff | Deeper option |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–2 min | Step out; drive a short marked loop to a visible dropped crate | Low-g movement, buggy bounce, first recovered object | Optional drift/jump route and a better photo viewpoint |
| 2–5 min | Put the first plan in the ground; send drones | Excavation visibly starts; home blueprint becomes real | Choose between two equally viable layouts |
| 5–9 min | Gather spoil and complete a single “make steel” work order | Fabricator visibly changes matter; first array unfolds | Learn why the chosen site gets morning/evening light |
| 9–13 min | Fit the ring and finish the first functional room | Pressure chime, warm light, VESPER reacting to actual completion | Compare garden/bunk/store adjacency without mandatory arithmetic |
| 13–20 min | Pick one expedition: ice observation, historical salvage or ridge route | A distinct short challenge and a thing brought home | Optional bonus objective rewards planning or driving skill |
| 20–30 min | Add the expedition's visible reward to home; prepare for dusk | A sprout, lamp, display or improved machine; sunset over a changed place | Power reserve target, efficient route or better layout |

The order can flex, but every segment should alternate a decision, a physical action and a visible consequence. Never ask for ten minutes of investment before demonstrating why the work matters.

## Beautiful and fun through the same changes

- **Make work visible.** Completed production fills a physical rack; active machines have distinct procedural movement, heat and light; drones visibly carry loads; a powered array's status reads from a glance. Functional beauty also reduces explanation.
- **Let the base acquire a silhouette.** Light-pipe crowns, named rooms, antennae, a crop canopy, a specimen display and a viewing perch communicate what has been achieved. Preserve an unobstructed vista and cluster purposeful detail around places the player uses.
- **Animate the schematic as a home.** The existing cross-section can show bunks, moving service lights, growing plants and drones crossing compartments. Give a completed room a close view and a cosmetic choice. This respects the no-walkable-hab doctrine while making ownership tangible.
- **Give emptiness contrast.** Keep long pristine views, but design the first area with a recognisable route, shelter, unusual outcrop, recoverable historical machine and a safe driving bowl. Procedural scenery should support an action or a meaningful destination.
- **Turn discoveries into possessions and stories.** A mineral sample displayed at home, a planted seed growing between visits, or a restored mission plaque provides a lasting reward without inflating material currency.

## One game for younger players and adults

Avoid treating age as a single difficulty slider. Reading fluency, motor control, fear tolerance and appetite for planning vary independently. Recommended settings: guided objectives, driving assistance, survival pressure, reading support and horror intensity. A confident child may want harder driving; an adult may want unhurried building.

For the accessible default: one concrete next goal, icon + short verb, optional spoken reading, forgiving snap placement, explicit material-source feedback, selectable inventory items, a visible route home, full pause for menus and an easy rescue. The brief “make a room, power it, bring something home” must be understandable without reading the entire Landfall Orders sheet.

For depth: let players optimise routes, payloads, grid schedules, layout, resource diversity and optional discovery objectives. Reward ingenuity with capability, visual personalisation and novel places. Do not add mandatory grind, punishment multipliers or a competitive score to every action. Keep a relaxed creative path valid alongside the mystery.

## Prioritisation and proof

**First: remove broken promises.** Fix construction material reach, refining automation/wording, resource/refund loss, and rescue/suit behaviour. Add one end-to-end fresh-save journey that does not inject machines or inventory past constraints. Current `scripts/live-hop.mjs:46-56` directly sets `hopperBuilt` and fuel, so it verifies the flight but cannot establish that a player can build the industry and manufacture replacement fuel after consuming the starting supply.

**Second: make the first session enjoyable.** Ship the compact landing→first useful home→short expedition loop, bench work orders, readable material handling, clear goal/status feedback and a visible home reward. Keep the current economy small while measuring friction.

**Third: widen play styles.** Add optional driving routes, personalisation, crop/specimen loops, assistance settings and alternative expedition purposes. Expand cave/story content only after return-to-home feels rewarding.

Test with observed fresh players aged roughly 8–10 and adults, including people who do not play crafting games. Measure whether they can identify the current goal and next action, reach first construction unaided, recover from a mistake, understand why a build was rejected, and finish a short expedition. Record voluntary return to the buggy/home and requests to keep playing. These observations establish fun and legibility; code gates establish the mechanical guarantees.
